The Timeline of Failure

Every broken promise, missed deadline and act of spectacular institutional cowardice — in order

April 2019

Bridge Closes After Cracks Found in Cast-Iron Pedestals

H&F Council closes the bridge to motor traffic after thermal monitoring reveals cracks in the cast-iron pedestals had worsened. Cyclists and pedestrians can still cross. The repair cost at this stage is estimated at around £46 million.

H&F Council Engineers
August 2020

Emergency Closure: Even Pedestrians Banned

H&F Council closes the bridge entirely — to pedestrians, cyclists, and river traffic — after further deterioration is found. This is the nadir. The bridge is now completely out of action.

H&F Council Engineers
August 2020

TfL Pledges £25 Million

Transport for London announces it will contribute £25 million toward the repair costs. This pledge will later prove to be somewhat optimistic in terms of what actually arrives.

TfL
October 2020

DfT Declares Bridge "Not Eligible" for Infrastructure Funding

The Department for Transport rules that Hammersmith Bridge does not qualify for national infrastructure funding — even though it is a listed structure of national importance carrying tens of thousands of daily journeys. The reasoning: it is a local bridge, therefore a local responsibility.

DfT
April 2021

Pedestrians Allowed Back

After stabilisation works, H&F Council reopens the bridge to pedestrians. A temporary walkway is installed. Cyclists remain banned. River traffic partially restored.

H&F Council
July 2021

Three-Way Cost-Sharing Agreement Reached

After months of negotiations, H&F Council, TfL, and the DfT agree to split the repair costs equally — one third each. At the time this feels like progress. It will later prove to be largely aspirational on the part of two of the three parties.

H&F Council TfL DfT
November 2021

DfT Suggests Demolishing the Bridge

Internal documents reveal that the Department for Transport considered recommending the complete demolition of Hammersmith Bridge and replacement with a new structure. The Grade II* listed, 138-year-old Victorian suspension bridge. Demolish it. This option was ultimately not pursued, presumably because someone pointed out it would be insane.

DfT
November 2021

Cyclists Finally Allowed Back — Via Temporary Platform

A temporary cycling platform is installed alongside the bridge, allowing cyclists to cross again. It is narrow, slightly alarming, and will remain in place for years longer than anyone expected.

H&F Council
March 2022

Stabilisation Works Begin

Physical repair work finally starts — the stabilisation phase, designed to stop the bridge getting worse before full strengthening can happen. This is phase one of two. Phase two (strengthening, allowing motors back) still has no funding confirmed.

H&F Council Engineers
Late 2022

TfL Drops the Bridge from Its Business Plan

Transport for London's 2024 Business Plan, published in late 2022, contains no funding for Hammersmith Bridge. Not reduced funding. Not deferred funding. The bridge simply isn't in it. TfL had pledged £25M in 2020 and had by this point contributed approximately £2.9M of that pledge.

TfL
2023

Cost Estimate Balloons to £141 Million. Then More.

The estimated cost of full repair — stabilisation plus strengthening — rises to £141 million, up from the original £46M estimate in 2019. It will subsequently rise further still, to over £250 million, as the scope of work and inflation take their toll.

Three Inquiries, Two Task Forces, One Very Tired Council

Parliamentary debates are held. A Hammersmith Bridge Task Force is convened, dissolved, and reconvened. Select committees ask questions. Ministers give non-committal answers. The bridge remains closed to motors. H&F Council continues to advance funds from its own reserves.

Wandsworth's position is that the bridge is H&F's responsibility. H&F notes that Wandsworth residents also use the bridge. This exchange produces no money.

Albert Bridge — the ornate pink suspension bridge half a mile upstream — is closed to motor vehicles after inspectors find a cracked cast-iron component. Pedestrians and cyclists can still use it. Albert Bridge joins Hammersmith Bridge as a closed Thames crossing. Two down.

28 March 2026

Wandsworth's Secret Sabotage Exposed: "No Money" — Then £870M for Housing

Department for Transport notes from a ministerial meeting in January 2025, published by Putney.news, reveal that when H&F Council leader offered to open talks about sharing the bridge repair costs, Wandsworth's representative refused on the spot. The response, as recorded by DfT officials: just two words — "no money." The representative then disputed H&F's traffic figures before leaving. Wandsworth then said nothing publicly for a year.

The punchline: Wandsworth Council has since approved plans to borrow £870 million for housing — including a single £65M land purchase at Battersea Power Station. Council reserves at the time stood at just under £200M. And three days before the DfT meeting, Wandsworth's own council leader Simon Hogg, transport cabinet member Jenny Yates, and Putney MP Fleur Anderson had stood together at a public rally in Putney demanding urgent action on the bridge — before going into the meeting and refusing to contribute a penny.

Where things stand: Seventh anniversary of closure arrives 10 April 2026. Structures Fund decision still pending. £870M available for housing in Wandsworth. £0 offered for the bridge. The gap between public rallying and private refusing has never been wider.

Late April 2026

Three More London Bridges Join the Crisis: Westminster, Lambeth and Vauxhall on TfL's "Critical" List

While Hammersmith and Fulham prepares its Structures Fund bid, Transport for London has published condition assessments revealing that three more Thames crossings are in "very poor condition." Westminster Bridge scored 21 out of 100; Lambeth Bridge and Vauxhall Bridge each scored 23. TfL's own threshold for triggering a formal investigation is 40 — so all three have cleared it with room to spare, in the wrong direction. The combined repair bill over the next decade is estimated at £85–130 million. None face immediate closure.

To recap the state of London's Thames crossings: Hammersmith Bridge has been shut to motor traffic since April 2019. Albert Bridge closed to motor traffic in February 2026 after inspectors found a cracked cast-iron component. And now Westminster, Lambeth and Vauxhall are on TfL's critical list. That £1 billion Structures Fund — the pot H&F is racing to bid into by August — must cover crumbling bridges, flyovers and tunnels across all of England. London's needs alone are beginning to look like an awkward competitor for their own prize.

Where things stand: Two London bridges shut to motor traffic. Three more on TfL's critical list. H&F's draft Structures Fund bid is due 19 June 2026; final submission 3 August; decision expected in autumn. The queue for the £1 billion just got longer.

12 May 2026

Vauxhall Bridge Gets Safety-Critical Weight Restrictions — TfL's "Long-Term Plan" Pending

Three weeks after Vauxhall Bridge appeared on TfL's "very poor condition" list with a score of 23 out of 100, the transport authority has announced something more concrete: a safety-critical 18-tonne weight limit, coming into force on 1 July 2026. TfL says a recent inspection found "some elements of the structure have deteriorated" — apparently more than the earlier condition score had captured. Buses and emergency vehicles are exempt. The roughly 200 heavy goods vehicles crossing each day (0.5% of Vauxhall's 39,500 daily crossings) will be diverted. TfL says it is "developing a long-term plan for the bridge's future."

Vauxhall Bridge opened in 1906. It joins Hammersmith (closed to motors since April 2019) and Albert Bridge (closed to motors since February 2026) as Thames crossings now operating at reduced or zero capacity for heavy vehicles. TfL's Director of Streets framed the restriction as protecting the bridge "while we develop the long-term improvements it needs" — which implies, tactfully, that the improvements are not yet decided, and that without the restriction the range of options might have begun to narrow on its own. The phrase "long-term plan" should by now carry a certain resonance for anyone following this saga.

Where things stand: Two Thames crossings fully closed to motors; a third restricted from July. H&F's draft Structures Fund bid is due 19 June — the £1 billion pot now notionally serves four deteriorating London Thames crossings alongside the rest of England. Vauxhall's long-term plan: still being developed.

12 May 2026

Putney Bridge Has Been Below the National Intervention Threshold Since 2023 — Wandsworth Knew and Did Nothing

Putney.news has published Wandsworth Council's own inspection data, revealing that Putney Bridge's critical load-bearing elements scored 28 out of 100. The national threshold at which the inspection manual requires action is 40. Wandsworth has known this since December 2023 — a year and a half ago. The bridge has no maintenance budget, no forward plan, and nothing has been done to it since January 2021.

The structural problems are specific: metal tie bars installed to strengthen the bridge are corroding, with nearly a third of their material gone in the worst areas. Cracks 2–5mm wide have opened where the central piers meet the arch. Water is getting through the road surface into the fabric of the structure, driving the corrosion and cracking. Putney Bridge opened in 1886. It now carries approximately 37,000 vehicles a day — around 16% more than before Hammersmith Bridge closed in 2019.

The particular richness of the situation is this: Putney Bridge is now the last fully unrestricted Thames crossing in southwest London. Hammersmith has been shut to motors since April 2019; Albert Bridge closed to motors in February 2026 and briefly to all users in April after sensors detected structural movement. And Putney Bridge belongs to Wandsworth Council — the authority that said "no money" at the DfT meeting in January 2025, approved £870 million in housing borrowing, and stood at a public rally demanding urgent bridge action before going inside and refusing to contribute a penny. Wandsworth has now applied to the Structures Fund — for its own bridge.

Where things stand: Putney Bridge has been below the national intervention threshold for over 18 months, its council having taken no action. H&F and Wandsworth are now both bidding into the same £1 billion Structures Fund, with draft applications due 19 June 2026. The last fully open crossing in southwest London is carrying 16% more traffic than before Hammersmith closed, scored 28/100 by its own council's engineers, and has no repair plan in place.

6 June 2026

Engineers Allege Collapse Warnings Were Dismissed in 2011 — Eight Years Before the Bridge Closed

An investigation published by The Londoner on 6 June 2026 reports that engineers raised concerns about Hammersmith Bridge's structural integrity as far back as 2011 — eight years before the bridge finally closed to motor traffic in April 2019. Those concerns were allegedly dismissed by the council's then-leadership. The engineers claim that had action been taken at the time, the deterioration now driving a repair bill cited in the investigation at £300 million would have been substantially mitigated.

The headline of the investigation says it plainly: "The real reason Hammersmith Bridge is closed." If the engineers' account is accurate, the real reason is not unforeseen Victorian ironwork or an unfortunate discovery of cracks. It is that engineers flagged the problem in 2011 — when it was still fixable at a fraction of the current cost — and were sent away. Eight years of compounding deterioration later, a team of engineers rushed into a meeting with council leader Stephen Cowan on 10 April 2019, giving him 30 minutes to decide whether to close the bridge before it risked catastrophic collapse. He closed it. The bridge has been shut ever since.

Where things stand: An exclusive investigation alleges engineers warned of structural failure in 2011 and were dismissed by the council's then-leadership — adding a new dimension to a crisis that is, it turns out, even more preventable than it appeared. The repair bill is now cited at up to £300 million. The Structures Fund draft bid deadline is 19 June 2026; a decision on funding is expected in autumn 2026.

16 June 2026

TfL Completes £31M Structural Works at Lambeth Bridge — Having Contributed £2.9M to Hammersmith in Seven Years

Transport for London has completed a £31 million programme of works at Lambeth Bridge, carried out by Tarmac Kier Joint Venture over seventeen months. The scope was genuinely structural: waterproofing, bridge deck repairs, and replacing temporary security barriers with permanent heritage-appropriate fixtures — alongside junction safety upgrades that swapped the roundabouts at both ends for signal-controlled junctions, installed protected cycle lanes on the bridge deck, and added new pedestrian crossings. The northern junction of Lambeth Bridge had been recorded as the most dangerous cycling location in London, with 22 of 31 cycling incidents in the capital between September 2021 and August 2024 concentrated there.

One number sits awkwardly beside the £31M: it is more than ten times the £2.9M TfL has contributed to Hammersmith Bridge since April 2019 — seven years in which TfL pledged £25M, delivered £2.9M, separately spent £16.7M on planning that led nowhere, and then dropped Hammersmith from its Business Plan entirely. Lambeth Bridge is a Grade II listed Thames crossing that TfL owns. Hammersmith Bridge is a Grade II* listed Thames crossing that TfL does not own, and the distinction appears, in practice, to be rather important.

The £31M comes with a caveat. Lambeth was rated 23 out of 100 in TfL's recent structural condition assessment — in the "very poor" category alongside Westminster (21) and Vauxhall (23). The estimated repair bill across all three crossings over the next decade remains £85–130 million. The completed works address the urgent maintenance backlog. The deeper structural liabilities do not appear to have changed.

Where things stand: TfL has completed £31M of structural maintenance and safety works at a bridge it owns — more than ten times its total contribution to Hammersmith Bridge over seven years. Lambeth's structural condition score (23/100) and the combined outstanding repair bill for three critical Thames crossings (£85–130M) remain. H&F's Structures Fund draft bid was due 19 June; final submission 3 August; decision expected in autumn 2026.

29 June 2026

Albert Bridge Begins Physical Repairs, Four Months After Closing — Hammersmith Enters Year Eight Still Unfunded

Physical repair work has started at Albert Bridge. Royal Borough of Kensington and Chelsea contractors began grit blasting the rocker bearings in the northern abutment chambers on Monday 29 June — described by RBKC as "the first stage of physically repairing these damaged components." Albert Bridge has been closed to motor traffic since 7 February 2026, when a cracked cast-iron component was found in a routine inspection. A temporary prop was installed in April to reduce the risk of further closures, and pedestrians and cyclists were allowed back shortly after. Motor traffic reopening is now targeted for 2027.

The comparison with Hammersmith Bridge does not require much amplification. Albert Bridge closed in February. Physical repairs started in June. Four months from closure to structural construction — because RBKC owns the bridge, decided to fix it, and fixed it. Motor traffic is expected back within roughly a year of the closure. Hammersmith Bridge, by contrast, closed to motor vehicles in April 2019. It has no confirmed funding for full repair. Its Structures Fund application (final submission due 3 August, decision expected autumn) is the only live funding path. If that application succeeds and works complete by the March 2030 deadline required by the fund, the full closure will have lasted eleven years. Albert Bridge's projected repair timeline, closure to reopening: approximately twelve months.

The structural situations are not identical. Albert Bridge's cracked cast-iron component was identified quickly and addressed with known techniques; Hammersmith's pedestal failures are more complex and its repair cost has grown from £46M in 2019 to over £250M. But the underlying difference — a single accountable owner versus a three-party funding dispute that has run for seven years — goes some distance to explaining why one bridge is being repaired and the other is still waiting for anyone to agree to pay for it.

Where things stand: Albert Bridge's physical repair is underway, with motor reopening targeted for 2027 — roughly twelve months after closure. Hammersmith Bridge has been shut to motor vehicles for over seven years; its Structures Fund bid (final submission 3 August, decision autumn 2026) remains the only confirmed route to full repair funding. Vauxhall Bridge's 18-tonne weight restriction, announced in May, comes into force on 1 July 2026.

30 June 2026 LATEST

H&F Quietly Downgrades Its Own Ambition: Latest Update Prioritises Keeping the Bridge Open, Not Reopening It — Wandsworth Calls It "Deeply Disappointing"

One day after Albert Bridge's four-month closure-to-construction turnaround made Hammersmith's timeline look worse than ever, H&F Council published its own update on the bridge's "phased" reopening plan — and the phasing has quietly changed shape. The council's stated priority is now the "immediate aim of keeping the bridge open and safeguarding access for pedestrians, cyclists and river traffic." Reopening to motor traffic — the entire point of the £250 million double-decker truss scheme designed by Foster + Partners and COWI — is notably absent from that sentence. Seven years in, the council's near-term ambition has shrunk from "fix it" to "don't let it get worse."

Wandsworth Council's cabinet member for transport, Cllr Dan Hamilton, did not take it well: "This latest update on Hammersmith Bridge is deeply disappointing. Instead of moving us closer to reopening, it locks in years more of congestion across south-west London. This will do further harm to residents and businesses and put even more strain on local transport networks." It is, notably, a more energetic public statement than anything Wandsworth managed at the January 2025 meeting where it told H&F "no money" and walked out — but consistency was never the strong suit on this file.

The context makes the downgrade look less like caution and more like arithmetic. H&F's final Structures Fund bid is due 3 August 2026, with a decision expected only in autumn — and the Hammersmith Bridge Taskforce, the one forum meant to resolve the funding standoff, has confirmed it will not reconvene until after those Structures Fund awards are made. There is, in short, nothing left to decide until autumn at the earliest, so the council has downgraded its own headline commitment to match. H&F has spent over £50 million since 2019 just keeping the bridge safe for pedestrians and cyclists, at a running cost of roughly £2 million a year — money that buys stability, not a reopening.

Where things stand: H&F's own latest update names "keeping the bridge open" — not reopening it to cars — as its immediate priority. The £250M double-decker truss scheme has no confirmed funding and no timeline. The Structures Fund final bid deadline is 3 August 2026; a decision is expected in autumn; the Taskforce will not meet again until after that decision lands. Wandsworth calls the delay "deeply disappointing." Nobody on either side of the river disputes it.

What If They'd Just Fixed It?

Counterfactuals for the bridge that bureaucracy forgot

If fixed in 2019…

At the original £46M estimate, splitting costs three ways, each party would have paid ~£15M. H&F has already spent £43M+ alone. A decision in 2019 would have saved the council approximately £28M — plus seven years of alternative transport costs for 22,000 daily journeys.

If fixed in 2021…

The three-way agreement was reached in July 2021 at an estimated £120M. Split equally, ~£40M each. TfL has paid £2.9M. The DfT has paid £8.3M. H&F has paid £43M+. Two of three parties have dramatically underpaid their agreed share.

Cost of inaction…

Every year of closure: 22,000 daily journeys diverted, ferry services subsidised, local businesses affected, residents adding 20–40 minutes to commutes. The £250M repair bill now exceeds the original estimate by over 5×. Delay is expensive.
